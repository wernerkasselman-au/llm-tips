# High-Signal Writing Guide

Evidence-based reference for prose, documentation, articles, and code comments that carry ownership, specificity, and observational grit instead of the statistical fingerprints of instruction-tuned models.

## Scope

Four domains where low-signal writing is most damaging: (1) published prose (articles, blogs, essays, marketing), (2) technical documentation (specs, READMEs, guides), (3) code comments, and (4) commit messages / PR descriptions / issue comments. Applies to human writing, LLM drafts, and human-edited LLM drafts.

## Principle

The goal isn't detector evasion. The goal is prose free of the uniform, risk-averse, noun-heavy, rhetorically predictable voice that models produce when trained to please raters. That voice lacks the specific observational grit that makes human writing worth reading.

## How to use

Greenfield: internalise §2 and §3, then run the §14 checklist before publishing. AI-assisted drafts: treat §4–§10 as grep targets. Ten or more hits → rewrite from the notes, don't sentence-edit. That's almost always faster (§12.2).

## Evidence base

Every rule cites at least one quantitative source. Core corpus: Reinhart, Brown et al. (PNAS 2025, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2422455122); Kobak et al. (Scientometrics 2024 / arXiv:2406.07016); Juzek & Ward (COLING 2025); Freeburg (arXiv:2603.27006); McGovern et al. (GenAIDetect 2025); Zamaraeva et al. (ACL 2025 Long Papers); Washington Post analysis of 328 744 ChatGPT messages (Merrill, Chen & Kumer, 13 Nov 2025); Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing; Sam Kriss, “Why Does A.I. Write Like… That?” (NYT Magazine, 3 Dec 2025). Full references in §15.
